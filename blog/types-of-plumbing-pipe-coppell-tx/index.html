Plumbing Pipe Types Found in Coppell, TX Homes

Plumbing pipe comes in several materials, and each ages and fails in its own way. A Coppell, TX home may have copper supply lines, plastic drain pipe and, in a few older spots, cast iron or galvanized steel. Knowing which you have tells you what to watch for and what to ask a plumber.

This guide separates supply pipe, which carries clean water in, from drain pipe, which carries waste out, because the materials and the problems differ.

Supply pipes: copper, CPVC, PEX and galvanized

Copper has been used for supply lines for decades and lasts a long time. It can develop pinhole leaks from corrosion, and a hard freeze can split it. CPVC is a cream or yellow plastic used in many homes built in the 1980s and later, and it can become brittle with age. PEX is flexible plastic that is common in newer work and in repipes. Galvanized steel is the oldest of the group and rusts from the inside.

Drain pipes: PVC, ABS and cast iron

Most newer drain pipe is white PVC or black ABS plastic. Older homes may have cast iron drain pipe, which is heavy and long lived but corrodes from the inside and can crack. Cast iron is often found under slabs in older houses and in the main stack.

Clay sewer pipe was used for outside sewer laterals in older areas. It can crack and let roots in at the joints.

How the material affects repair

A leak in copper is repaired by cutting out the section and soldering or using a push fitting. A leak in PEX is repaired with a crimp or expansion fitting. CPVC needs a solvent cement joint. Galvanized pipe is hard to repair because the threads are corroded, so replacement is the more common answer.

Mixing metals needs care. Joining copper directly to galvanized steel can cause corrosion where they touch, so a dielectric fitting is used.

Signs your pipe is aging out

One leak does not mean the whole house needs new pipe. A pattern does. If you see several of these signs, ask about repiping and compare it to repairs.

  • Several pinhole leaks within a short time.
  • Rust colored water that does not clear.
  • Falling pressure at fixtures.
  • Green or white crust on pipe joints.
  • Pipe that crumbles or flakes when touched.

Polybutylene and other known problem materials

Some houses built in certain decades used gray plastic called polybutylene for supply lines. It is known for failing at fittings. If you find gray flexible pipe with plastic fittings, have a plumber look at it. Replacement is common because failures can be sudden.

What a plumber checks first

We identify the material at the meter, near the water heater and under sinks. We test water pressure at a hose bib and look for corrosion at joints. If you want a record of what you have, ask for photos. See plumbing installation for related work.

How to photograph and record your pipes

If you are planning repairs or a sale, a quick photo record of your pipes is useful. Take a picture of the main shutoff, the pipe at the water heater, the pipe under each sink and any exposed pipe in the garage or attic. Note the color and whether the joints are soldered, threaded or crimped.

Bring those photos to a plumber and you will get a faster, better answer. They also help an insurer or a buyer understand what is in the house. If you cannot tell what a pipe is, ask. It is better to know.

Questions about pipe age in Texas homes

Homeowners often ask how long each pipe lasts. The answer depends on the material, the water and the installation. Copper often lasts decades, CPVC can become brittle sooner and galvanized steel is usually the first to need replacement. PEX is newer, so it has a shorter record, but it has performed well in repipes.

What matters more than a number is the condition. A pipe that looks clean, holds pressure and has no green crust is likely fine. A pipe that leaks at several joints or shows rust is telling you it is near the end.

Coppell notes on pipe age and soil

Coppell grew after DFW Airport opened in 1974 and was largely built out by about 2000, so houses range from the 1980s to the 2000s with newer pockets such as Cypress Waters. The pipe in your home depends on its build year, not on the city. Ask the year and then look at the pipe itself.

The soil under Coppell is expansive clay, which swells and shrinks according to the Dallas County Master Gardeners. That movement can stress pipes in the slab and buried lines. If you have neighbors in Irving or Carrollton, their older housing may include more cast iron and galvanized pipe.
