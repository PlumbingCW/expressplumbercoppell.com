How Plumbing Tape Works: A Coppell, TX Homeowner Guide

Plumbing tape, also called thread seal tape or Teflon tape, helps a threaded pipe joint seal. It is a thin white film that fills the tiny gaps between male and female threads so water does not slip through. This Coppell, TX homeowner guide explains how it works, how to wrap it and where it should not be used.

Used correctly, tape is a cheap way to stop a weeping threaded joint. Used in the wrong place, it hides a problem.

What plumbing tape does and does not do

Threads do not seal on their own. They have small spiral gaps. Tape is a soft film made from a slippery plastic. As you tighten the joint, the film squeezes into the gaps and acts as a gasket and a lubricant. It also helps the joint turn without galling, which can ruin threads.

It does not fix a cracked pipe or a damaged thread. If the joint leaks after you wrap it correctly and tighten it, something else is wrong.

Where tape belongs and where it does not

Use tape on tapered pipe threads, the kind on water supply nipples, shutoff valves and hose bibs. Do not use it on compression fittings, flare fittings or on flat washers, since those seal with a gasket or a ferrule. Tape on a fitting that seals with a washer can keep it from closing properly.

How to wrap it

More wraps is not better. Too much tape can crack a female fitting or keep the threads from seating.

  1. Turn off the water and dry the threads.
  2. Hold the male thread facing you.
  3. Wrap the tape clockwise as you look at the end, so tightening does not unwrap it.
  4. Use two to three layers on most threads.
  5. Press the tape into the threads with your thumb.
  6. Tighten the joint by hand, then a quarter to a half turn with a wrench.
  7. Turn the water on slowly and check for drips.

Types of tape

White tape is the standard for water. Pink tape is thicker and often used for water on larger threads. Yellow tape is made for gas. Pipe dope, a paste, is another option and some plumbers use both on larger joints. Choose the product rated for your application.

Mistakes that cause leaks

  • Wrapping counterclockwise so it bunches up as you tighten.
  • Using too much tape on a small thread.
  • Taping a compression fitting.
  • Overtightening a plastic fitting and cracking it.
  • Reusing old tape instead of removing it.

When to call a plumber instead

If a joint still weeps after one careful retry, if the fitting is corroded or if the leak is behind a wall, call a plumber. A leaking joint on a gas line is a different story. Leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 before anything else. For small fixture jobs, faucet and sink repair and plumbing installation cover what we do.

A closer look at pipe dope versus tape

Pipe dope is a paste that fills thread gaps and stays slightly soft. Tape is a film. Many plumbers like dope on larger threads and on metal fittings because it fills and lubricates at once. Tape is cleaner and easier on small threads. On plastic fittings, use a sealant that is rated for plastic, since some pastes can crack it.

Whatever you choose, apply it to the male thread only. If you put it on the inside of a female fitting, bits can end up in the line and clog an aerator or a valve.

Signs a taped joint is failing

If you see any of these signs, shut off the water, take the joint apart and look at the threads before you retape. A worn thread needs a new part, not more tape.

  • A slow drip at the threads that returns after you tighten.
  • A white crust around the joint from mineral deposits.
  • Fine shreds of tape in a faucet aerator.
  • A fitting that has been overtightened and cracked.

A short guide to tightening threaded fittings

Over-tightening is the other half of a leaking threaded joint. Metal fittings should be snug by hand and then turned one to two more turns with a wrench, depending on size. Plastic fittings should be hand tight plus a quarter to a half turn. More force can crack plastic and strip metal threads.

Always support the pipe on the other side with a second wrench when you tighten, so you do not twist the pipe inside the wall. A twisted pipe can crack a soldered joint behind the drywall, and then you have a leak you cannot see.
